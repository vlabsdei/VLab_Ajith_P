This experiment covers the full aerodynamic picture of the drone. ## 1. Lift Coefficient vs Angle of Attack (Sub-Calc A)

The lift generated by an airfoil is characterized by the Lift Coefficient (<i>C</i><sub>l</sub>), a dimensionless number representing the lifting capability of the blade section. According to thin airfoil theory, the lift coefficient varies linearly with the Angle of Attack (<i>&alpha;</i>) until stall:

<p align="center"><b><i>C</i><sub>l</sub> = 2&pi;(<i>&alpha;</i> &minus; <i>&alpha;</i><sub>0</sub>)</b></p>

Where:
* <i>C</i><sub>l</sub> is the Lift Coefficient, a dimensionless number representing the lifting capability of the airfoil.
* <i>&alpha;</i> (alpha) is the Angle of Attack, the angle between the oncoming air and the airfoil's chord line.
* <i>&alpha;</i><sub>0</sub> is the zero-lift angle of attack, derived from standard NACA TR-824 wind tunnel data.

> **Note on slope:** The theoretical lift-curve slope is 2&pi; per **radian** &asymp; **0.1097 per degree**. This means each 1&deg; increase in angle of attack adds approximately 0.11 to <i>C</i><sub>l</sub> in the linear regime (e.g., at <i>&alpha;</i> = 5&deg; for NACA 0012: <i>C</i><sub>l</sub> = 2&pi; &times; 5 &times; &pi;/180 &asymp; 0.55).

> **Worked example — lift coefficient of a NACA 2412 section at <i>&alpha;</i> = 5&deg;.** Using the zero-lift angle <i>&alpha;</i><sub>0</sub> = &minus;2.0&deg; from the NACA airfoil table below:
> <p align="center"><i>C</i><sub>l</sub> = 2&pi;(<i>&alpha;</i> &minus; <i>&alpha;</i><sub>0</sub>) = 2&pi;(5&deg; &minus; (&minus;2&deg;)) = 2&pi; &times; 0.1222 rad = <b>0.768</b></p>
> This sits well below the section's <i>C</i><sub>l,max</sub> &asymp; 1.4 (stall at 14&deg;), so the flow stays fully attached and the linear thin-airfoil result is valid.

### NACA Airfoil Profiles

The NACA 4-digit airfoil designation describes the geometry:
* **First digit**: Maximum camber as percentage of chord (0 = symmetric)
* **Second digit**: Location of maximum camber from leading edge (tenths of chord)
* **Third & fourth digits**: Maximum thickness as percentage of chord

| Airfoil | Camber (%) | Camber Position | Thickness (%) | <i>&alpha;</i><sub>0</sub> (deg) |
|---------|------------|-----------------|---------------|------------------|
| NACA 0012 | 0 | N/A | 12 | 0.0 |
| NACA 2412 | 2 | 40% | 12 | -2.0 |
| NACA 4412 | 4 | 40% | 12 | -4.0 |

### Stall Characteristics

As the angle of attack increases, the lift coefficient increases linearly until the flow separates from the upper surface. This separation causes:
* **Stall**: A sudden loss of lift
* **Cl_max**: The maximum lift coefficient before stall
* **Stall angle**: The angle of attack at which Cl_max occurs

The heavily cambered NACA 4412 achieves a higher <i>C</i><sub>l,max</sub> &asymp; 1.6 compared to the symmetric NACA 0012 (<i>C</i><sub>l,max</sub> &asymp; 1.2), but stalls at a lower angle of attack.

> **Low-Re Stall Note:** The stall angles listed (e.g., NACA 0012 at 15&deg;) are based on high-Reynolds-number wind tunnel data (<i>Re</i> &gt; 1&times;10<sup>6</sup>). Drone propeller tips typically operate at <i>Re</i> = 50,000&ndash;200,000, where stall generally occurs earlier at 12&ndash;14&deg;. The curves in this experiment are high-Re educational approximations; see Section 5 for low-Reynolds number considerations.

## 2. Aerodynamic Drag (Sub-Calc B)

The aerodynamic drag force opposing the drone's forward motion is calculated using the drag equation:

<p align="center"><b><i>F</i><sub>D</sub> = 0.5 &middot; <i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>V</i><sup>2</sup> &middot; <i>C</i><sub>d</sub> &middot; <i>A</i><sub>frontal</sub></b></p>

Where:
* <i>F</i><sub>D</sub> is the Drag force opposing the drone's motion, measured in Newtons (N).
* <i>&rho;</i> (rho) is the air density (1.225 kg/m<sup>3</sup> at sea level).
* <i>V</i> is the wind speed or forward airspeed, measured in metres per second (m/s).
* <i>C</i><sub>d</sub> is the Drag coefficient. For a standard X-frame bluff body, this is approximated as **1.05** based on NASA TN-D-8236. **Note:** This value applies to the clean airframe body. In actual forward flight, rotor downwash turbulence and interference effects typically add 20&ndash;30% additional drag to the total system, not captured by this simplified body-only model.
* <i>A</i><sub>frontal</sub> is the frontal cross-sectional area of the drone.

> **Worked example — frame drag of the 250 mm build at top wind speed.** For the cf_250 frame (<i>A</i><sub>frontal</sub> = 0.0085 m<sup>2</sup>), the X-frame drag coefficient <i>C</i><sub>d</sub> = 1.05, sea-level <i>&rho;</i> = 1.225 kg/m<sup>3</sup> and the maximum tunnel speed <i>V</i> = 15 m/s:
> <p align="center"><i>F</i><sub>D</sub> = 0.5 &middot; <i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>V</i><sup>2</sup> &middot; <i>C</i><sub>d</sub> &middot; <i>A</i> = 0.5 &middot; 1.225 &middot; 15<sup>2</sup> &middot; 1.05 &middot; 0.0085 = <b>1.23 N</b></p>
> That is roughly 125 g-force of drag — modest at 15 m/s, but because <i>F</i><sub>D</sub> &prop; <i>V</i><sup>2</sup> it quadruples if the airspeed doubles.

### Quadratic Drag Relationship

The drag equation demonstrates that drag force scales with the **square of velocity**. This fundamental relationship means:
* Doubling speed quadruples drag
* Power required to overcome drag scales with <i>V</i><sup>3</sup>

When plotting <i>F</i><sub>D</sub> versus <i>V</i><sup>2</sup>, the relationship yields a perfectly linear regression with slope equal to 0.5 &middot; <i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>C</i><sub>d</sub> &middot; <i>A</i>.

## 3. Advance Ratio (Sub-Calc C)

The Advance Ratio (<i>J</i>) is a dimensionless parameter relating forward speed to propeller tip speed:

<p align="center"><b><i>J</i> = <sup><i>V</i></sup> &frasl; <sub>(<i>n</i> &middot; <i>D</i>)</sub></b></p>

Where:
* <i>J</i> is the Advance Ratio, a dimensionless parameter.
* <i>V</i> is the forward airspeed (m/s).
* <i>n</i> is the propeller rotational speed in revolutions per second (RPS).
* <i>D</i> is the propeller diameter in metres.

> **Worked example — advance ratio of the 5045 prop at 12,000 RPM.** Taking <i>n</i> = 200 RPS (12,000 RPM) for the 5045 prop (<i>D</i> = 0.127 m) at a forward speed <i>V</i> = 12 m/s:
> <p align="center"><i>J</i> = <sup><i>V</i></sup> &frasl; <sub>(<i>n</i> &middot; <i>D</i>)</sub> = <sup>12</sup> &frasl; <sub>(200 &times; 0.127)</sub> = <b>0.472</b></p>
> This is just below the propeller's optimal <i>J</i> &asymp; 0.55; reaching the peak-efficiency point at this RPM requires <i>V</i> = 0.55 &times; 200 &times; 0.127 &asymp; 13.97 m/s.

### Physical Interpretation

* **J = 0 (Hover)**: The propeller operates in static conditions with no forward motion.
* **J > 0 (Forward Flight)**: The propeller advances through the air, reducing the effective angle of attack.
* **High J**: The propeller may experience windmilling or thrust reversal.
* **Optimal J**: At the advance ratio where peak propulsive efficiency occurs, the effective blade angle of attack is at its most efficient point on the blade section's lift-to-drag polar — the blade operates closest to its design Cl/Cd maximum.

The advance ratio bridges the gap between static hover performance and forward cruise efficiency.

## 4. Propulsive Efficiency (Sub-Calc C)

Propulsive efficiency (<i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub>) measures how effectively the propeller converts rotational power into forward thrust:

<p align="center"><b><i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub> = <sup>(<i>T</i> &middot; <i>V</i>)</sup> &frasl; <sub>(2&pi; &middot; <i>n</i> &middot; <i>Q</i>)</sub></b></p>

Where:
* <i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub> is the Propulsive efficiency, expressed as a percentage (%).
* <i>T</i> is the Thrust (N).
* <i>V</i> is the Forward airspeed (m/s).
* <i>n</i> is the Rotational speed (RPS).
* <i>Q</i> is the Propeller torque (N&middot;m), derived via the torque coefficient.

### Torque Calculation

The propeller torque is calculated using:

<p align="center"><b><i>Q</i> = <i>C</i><sub>q</sub> &middot; <i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>n</i><sup>2</sup> &middot; <i>D</i><sup>5</sup></b></p>

Where <i>C</i><sub>q</sub> &asymp; 0.0122 is the torque coefficient for a standard 5045 propeller (calibrated to forward-flight propulsive efficiency — distinct from static-hover bench test values where induced inflow and tip-loss effects shift Cq; the forward-flight Cq yields a peak propulsive efficiency of &asymp; 78% at the optimal advance ratio).

> **Worked example — thrust, torque and efficiency at the optimal advance ratio.** Running the 5045 prop (<i>C</i><sub>t</sub> = 0.109, <i>C</i><sub>q</sub> = 0.01223, <i>D</i> = 0.127 m) at <i>n</i> = 200 RPS, with <i>V</i> = 13.97 m/s so that <i>J</i> = 0.55:
> <p align="center"><i>T</i> = <i>C</i><sub>t</sub> &middot; <i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>n</i><sup>2</sup> &middot; <i>D</i><sup>4</sup> = 0.109 &middot; 1.225 &middot; 200<sup>2</sup> &middot; 0.127<sup>4</sup> = <b>1.39 N</b></p>
> <p align="center"><i>Q</i> = <i>C</i><sub>q</sub> &middot; <i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>n</i><sup>2</sup> &middot; <i>D</i><sup>5</sup> = 0.01223 &middot; 1.225 &middot; 200<sup>2</sup> &middot; 0.127<sup>5</sup> = <b>0.0198 N&middot;m</b></p>
> <p align="center"><i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub> = <sup>(<i>T</i> &middot; <i>V</i>)</sup> &frasl; <sub>(2&pi; &middot; <i>n</i> &middot; <i>Q</i>)</sub> = <sup>(1.39 &times; 13.97)</sup> &frasl; <sub>(2&pi; &times; 200 &times; 0.0198)</sub> = <b>0.780 = 78.0%</b></p>
> The result lands exactly on the calibrated peak of &asymp; 78% for the 5045 at <i>J</i> &asymp; 0.55 — the propeller is operating at its most efficient cruise point.

### Efficiency Curve Characteristics

* **At Hover (V = 0)**: <i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub> = 0 (no useful work is done)
* **Peak Efficiency**: Occurs at optimal advance ratio (<i>J</i> &asymp; 0.55 for 5045 propeller)
* **Beyond Peak**: Efficiency drops as the advancing blade stalls

## 5. Low Reynolds Number Considerations

Small drone propellers operate in a low Reynolds number regime (<i>Re</i> &lt; 200,000) where viscous effects dominate. The Reynolds number is calculated as:

<p align="center"><b><i>Re</i> = <sup>(<i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>v</i><sub>tip</sub> &middot; <i>c</i><sub>mean</sub>)</sup> &frasl; <sub><i>&mu;</i></sub></b></p>

Where:
* <i>v</i><sub>tip</sub> = &pi; &middot; <i>n</i> &middot; <i>D</i> is the blade tip speed
* <i>c</i><sub>mean</sub> is the mean blade chord
* <i>&mu;</i> is the air dynamic viscosity

> **Worked example — blade-tip Reynolds number.** With <i>n</i> = 200 RPS, <i>D</i> = 0.127 m, mean blade chord <i>c</i><sub>mean</sub> = 0.012 m and <i>&mu;</i> = 1.81&times;10<sup>&minus;5</sup> Pa&middot;s:
> <p align="center"><i>v</i><sub>tip</sub> = &pi; &middot; <i>n</i> &middot; <i>D</i> = &pi; &times; 200 &times; 0.127 = <b>79.8 m/s</b></p>
> <p align="center"><i>Re</i> = <sup>(<i>&rho;</i> &middot; <i>v</i><sub>tip</sub> &middot; <i>c</i><sub>mean</sub>)</sup> &frasl; <sub><i>&mu;</i></sub> = <sup>(1.225 &times; 79.8 &times; 0.012)</sup> &frasl; <sub>1.81&times;10<sup>&minus;5</sup></sub> = <b>6.5&times;10<sup>4</sup></b></p>
> At <i>Re</i> &asymp; 65,000 the blade sits firmly in the low-Reynolds regime (<i>Re</i> &lt; 200,000), so viscous effects are significant and the high-Re lift curves should be treated as educational approximations.

### Reality Check: Low-Reynolds Dilemma

Thin airfoil theory breaks down near stall angles (<i>&alpha;</i> &gt; 12&deg;) and at low Reynolds numbers. At <i>Re</i> = 50,000 (typical propeller tip conditions):
* A symmetric airfoil might actually outperform a cambered one
* The curves provided are high-Re approximations
* Results should be treated as educational tools rather than direct design lookup charts

## 6. Rotor-Airframe Interference

The drag calculation treats the drone as a clean body in a wind tunnel. In actual forward flight:
* Rotors blast a turbulent downwash that interacts with the frame
* Effective frontal drag is typically **15-30% higher** than clean-body predictions
* This rotor-airframe interference is not captured in the basic drag equation

## 7. Cruise Power Budget

The total power required for forward cruise combines the propulsive power and the aerodynamic drag power:

<p align="center"><b><i>P</i><sub>cruise</sub> = <sup>(<i>T</i> &middot; <i>V</i>)</sup> &frasl; <sub><i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub></sub> + <i>F</i><sub>D</sub> &middot; <i>V</i></b></p>

Where <i>F</i><sub>D</sub> is the drag force from Sub-Calc B. The minimum total power cruise speed is found at d<i>P</i><sub>cruise</sub>/d<i>V</i> = 0, which occurs at a lower airspeed than the minimum drag speed. This cruise power budget will be used directly in Experiment 7 range and endurance calculations.

> **Worked example — cruise power budget of the reference quad.** At the <i>J</i> = 0.55 cruise point (<i>V</i> = 13.97 m/s, <i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub> = 0.78): each of the 4 props delivers <i>T</i> = 1.39 N (total thrust 4<i>T</i> = 5.56 N), and the cf_250 airframe drag at this speed is <i>F</i><sub>D</sub> = 0.5 &middot; 1.225 &middot; 13.97<sup>2</sup> &middot; 1.05 &middot; 0.0085 = 1.07 N:
> <p align="center"><i>P</i><sub>cruise</sub> = <sup>((4<i>T</i>) &middot; <i>V</i>)</sup> &frasl; <sub><i>&eta;</i><sub>prop</sub></sub> + <i>F</i><sub>D</sub> &middot; <i>V</i> = <sup>(5.56 &times; 13.97)</sup> &frasl; <sub>0.78</sub> + 1.07 &times; 13.97 = 99.5 + 14.9 = <b>114 W</b></p>
> About 114 W of shaft power sustains this cruise (99.5 W of propeller shaft power plus a 14.9 W parasitic-drag allowance) — the value carried forward into the Experiment 7 range and endurance budget.

## 8. Cross-Experiment Relationships

This experiment completes the aerodynamic characterisation of the drone in conjunction with Experiments 1 and 2:

* **Thrust coefficients (Ct):** The Ct values used in Sub-Calc C (propulsive efficiency) are the same physical propeller aerodynamic coefficients used in Experiment 1's propulsion engine. The values are consistent across all three experiments via the shared component database.
* **Frontal area (A_frontal):** The frontal area used for drag calculation in Sub-Calc B is a structural property of the frame selected in Experiment 2. Selecting a larger frame directly increases the drag force calculated here.
* **Drag output → Experiment 7:** The drag force calculated at cruise speed in Sub-Calc B feeds into Experiment 7's range and endurance calculations as the required cruise drag input. The calculated aerodynamic drag value (<i>F</i><sub>D</sub>) is stored as `vlabExp3_dragForce` in browser local storage for use in Experiment 7.
