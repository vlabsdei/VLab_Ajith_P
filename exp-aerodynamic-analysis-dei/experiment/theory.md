# Theoretical Background: Aerodynamic Analysis

This experiment covers the full aerodynamic picture of the drone. While previous experiments treated thrust as a simple vector, this module breaks down exactly how that thrust is generated by the propeller blade, how the drone's body opposes forward motion, and how efficiently the propeller converts rotational power into forward thrust.

---

## 1. Lift Coefficient vs Angle of Attack (Sub-Calc A)

The lift generated by an airfoil is characterized by the Lift Coefficient ($C_l$), a dimensionless number representing the lifting capability of the blade section. According to thin airfoil theory, the lift coefficient varies linearly with the Angle of Attack ($\alpha$) until stall:

$$C_l = 2\pi(\alpha - \alpha_0)$$

Where:
* $C_l$ is the Lift Coefficient, a dimensionless number representing the lifting capability of the airfoil.
* $\alpha$ (alpha) is the Angle of Attack, the angle between the oncoming air and the airfoil's chord line.
* $\alpha_0$ is the zero-lift angle of attack, derived from standard NACA TR-824 wind tunnel data.

> **Note on slope:** The theoretical lift-curve slope is $2\pi$ per **radian** ≈ **0.1097 per degree**. This means each 1° increase in angle of attack adds approximately 0.11 to $C_l$ in the linear regime (e.g., at $\alpha = 5°$ for NACA 0012: $C_l = 2\pi \times 5 \times \pi/180 \approx 0.55$).

### NACA Airfoil Profiles

The NACA 4-digit airfoil designation describes the geometry:
* **First digit**: Maximum camber as percentage of chord (0 = symmetric)
* **Second digit**: Location of maximum camber from leading edge (tenths of chord)
* **Third & fourth digits**: Maximum thickness as percentage of chord

| Airfoil | Camber (%) | Camber Position | Thickness (%) | $\alpha_0$ (deg) |
|---------|------------|-----------------|---------------|------------------|
| NACA 0012 | 0 | N/A | 12 | 0.0 |
| NACA 2412 | 2 | 40% | 12 | -2.0 |
| NACA 4412 | 4 | 40% | 12 | -4.0 |

### Stall Characteristics

As the angle of attack increases, the lift coefficient increases linearly until the flow separates from the upper surface. This separation causes:
* **Stall**: A sudden loss of lift
* **Cl_max**: The maximum lift coefficient before stall
* **Stall angle**: The angle of attack at which Cl_max occurs

The heavily cambered NACA 4412 achieves a higher $C_{l\_max} \approx 1.6$ compared to the symmetric NACA 0012 ($C_{l\_max} \approx 1.2$), but stalls at a lower angle of attack.

> **Low-Re Stall Note:** The stall angles listed (e.g., NACA 0012 at 15°) are based on high-Reynolds-number wind tunnel data (Re > 1×10⁶). Drone propeller tips typically operate at Re = 50,000–200,000, where stall generally occurs earlier at 12–14°. The curves in this experiment are high-Re educational approximations; see Section 5 for low-Reynolds number considerations.

## 2. Aerodynamic Drag (Sub-Calc B)

The aerodynamic drag force opposing the drone's forward motion is calculated using the drag equation:

$$F_D = 0.5 \cdot \rho \cdot V^2 \cdot C_d \cdot A_{frontal}$$

Where:
* $F_D$ is the Drag force opposing the drone's motion, measured in Newtons (N).
* $\rho$ (rho) is the air density ($1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$ at sea level).
* $V$ is the wind speed or forward airspeed, measured in metres per second (m/s).
* $C_d$ is the Drag coefficient. For a standard X-frame bluff body, this is approximated as **1.05** based on NASA TN-D-8236. **Note:** This value applies to the clean airframe body. In actual forward flight, rotor downwash turbulence and interference effects typically add 20–30% additional drag to the total system, not captured by this simplified body-only model.
* $A_{frontal}$ is the frontal cross-sectional area of the drone.

### Quadratic Drag Relationship

The drag equation demonstrates that drag force scales with the **square of velocity**. This fundamental relationship means:
* Doubling speed quadruples drag
* Power required to overcome drag scales with $V^3$

When plotting $F_D$ versus $V^2$, the relationship yields a perfectly linear regression with slope equal to $0.5 \cdot \rho \cdot C_d \cdot A$.

## 3. Advance Ratio (Sub-Calc C)

The Advance Ratio ($J$) is a dimensionless parameter relating forward speed to propeller tip speed:

$$J = \frac{V}{n \cdot D}$$

Where:
* $J$ is the Advance Ratio, a dimensionless parameter.
* $V$ is the forward airspeed (m/s).
* $n$ is the propeller rotational speed in revolutions per second (RPS).
* $D$ is the propeller diameter in metres.

### Physical Interpretation

* **J = 0 (Hover)**: The propeller operates in static conditions with no forward motion.
* **J > 0 (Forward Flight)**: The propeller advances through the air, reducing the effective angle of attack.
* **High J**: The propeller may experience windmilling or thrust reversal.
* **Optimal J**: At the advance ratio where peak propulsive efficiency occurs, the effective blade angle of attack is at its most efficient point on the blade section's lift-to-drag polar — the blade operates closest to its design Cl/Cd maximum.

The advance ratio bridges the gap between static hover performance and forward cruise efficiency.

## 4. Propulsive Efficiency (Sub-Calc C)

Propulsive efficiency ($\eta_{prop}$) measures how effectively the propeller converts rotational power into forward thrust:

$$\eta_{prop} = \frac{T \cdot V}{2\pi \cdot n \cdot Q}$$

Where:
* $\eta_{prop}$ is the Propulsive efficiency, expressed as a percentage (%).
* $T$ is the Thrust (N).
* $V$ is the Forward airspeed (m/s).
* $n$ is the Rotational speed (RPS).
* $Q$ is the Propeller torque (N·m), derived via the torque coefficient.

### Torque Calculation

The propeller torque is calculated using:

$$Q = C_q \cdot \rho \cdot n^2 \cdot D^5$$

Where $C_q \approx 0.0122$ is the torque coefficient for a standard 5045 propeller (calibrated to forward-flight propulsive efficiency — distinct from static-hover bench test values where induced inflow and tip-loss effects shift Cq; the forward-flight Cq yields a peak propulsive efficiency of ≈ 78% at the optimal advance ratio).

### Efficiency Curve Characteristics

* **At Hover (V = 0)**: $\eta_{prop} = 0$ (no useful work is done)
* **Peak Efficiency**: Occurs at optimal advance ratio ($J \approx 0.55$ for 5045 propeller)
* **Beyond Peak**: Efficiency drops as the advancing blade stalls

## 5. Low Reynolds Number Considerations

Small drone propellers operate in a low Reynolds number regime ($Re < 200,000$) where viscous effects dominate. The Reynolds number is calculated as:

$$Re = \frac{\rho \cdot v_{tip} \cdot c_{mean}}{\mu}$$

Where:
* $v_{tip} = \pi \cdot n \cdot D$ is the blade tip speed
* $c_{mean}$ is the mean blade chord
* $\mu$ is the air dynamic viscosity

### Reality Check: Low-Reynolds Dilemma

Thin airfoil theory breaks down near stall angles ($\alpha > 12°$) and at low Reynolds numbers. At $Re = 50,000$ (typical propeller tip conditions):
* A symmetric airfoil might actually outperform a cambered one
* The curves provided are high-Re approximations
* Results should be treated as educational tools rather than direct design lookup charts

## 6. Rotor-Airframe Interference

The drag calculation treats the drone as a clean body in a wind tunnel. In actual forward flight:
* Rotors blast a turbulent downwash that interacts with the frame
* Effective frontal drag is typically **15-30% higher** than clean-body predictions
* This rotor-airframe interference is not captured in the basic drag equation

---

## 7. Cruise Power Budget

The total power required for forward cruise combines the propulsive power and the aerodynamic drag power:

$$P_{cruise} = \frac{T \cdot V}{\eta_{prop}} + F_D \cdot V$$

Where $F_D$ is the drag force from Sub-Calc B. The minimum total power cruise speed is found at $dP_{cruise}/dV = 0$, which occurs at a lower airspeed than the minimum drag speed. This cruise power budget will be used directly in Experiment 7 range and endurance calculations.

This experiment completes the aerodynamic characterisation of the drone in conjunction with Experiments 1 and 2:
